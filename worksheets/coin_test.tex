\smallskip
{\large Invent a way to decide}

\medskip
Names: \rule{0.38\textwidth}{0.4pt} \hfill \rule{0.38\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\medskip
Work in pairs, with a pencil; about 30 minutes. No calculator needed.

\begin{highlightbox}
\textbf{The binomial distribution.}
Flip a coin $n$ times, independently, with probability $p$ of heads on every flip,
and count the heads, $X$.
Then $X$ has the \emph{binomial distribution}:
\[
  P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n,
\]
where $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the sequences of $n$ flips that contain exactly $k$ heads.
For a fair coin ($p = 1/2$) and $n = 10$, all $2^{10} = 1024$ sequences are equally likely,
so $P(X = k) = \binom{10}{k} / 1024$:
the number of sequences with $k$ heads, out of 1024 (chart and table below).
\end{highlightbox}

\medskip
\begin{highlightbox}
A dealer invites you to bet on a coin and claims it is fair.
You may watch 10 flips; then you must decide whether to accuse the dealer of using an unfair coin.
\textbf{Before any coin is flipped, plan how you will decide.}
\end{highlightbox}

\question{Q1.}
If the dealer is honest, which numbers of heads would you expect in 10 flips?
Which numbers would make you suspect the dealer? Justify your choice.
\answerbox[1.7cm]{Expect about 5; 4 to 6 heads cover 672 of 1024 sequences, about two thirds.
Suspicious: results that are rare for a fair coin, such as 0, 1, 9, or 10 heads (22 of 1024, about 2\%).}

\question{Q2.}
Write your plan: which numbers of heads will lead you to accuse the dealer?
\answerbox[1.0cm]{For example: accuse if there are 0, 1, 9, or 10 heads.}

\question{Q3.}
If the dealer is honest, how often would your plan wrongly accuse them? Count sequences in the table.
\answerbox[1.2cm]{For $\{0, 1, 9, 10\}$: $1 + 10 + 10 + 1 = 22$ of 1024, about 2\%.
For $\{0, 1, 2, 8, 9, 10\}$: 112 of 1024, about 11\%.}

\question{Q4.}
Here is a limit we give you: wrongly accuse an honest dealer at most 1 time in 20
(5\%, about 51 of the 1024 sequences).
Adjust your plan to accuse on as many numbers of heads as that limit allows.
\answerbox[1.3cm]{Accuse on 0, 1, 9, or 10 heads: 22 sequences, 2.1\%.
Adding 2 and 8 makes 112 sequences, 10.9\%, over the limit.
A plan that accuses on a fixed list of head counts cannot hit 5\% exactly.}

\question{Q5.}
Why make the plan before seeing any flips?
\answerbox[1.2cm]{Otherwise we could always pick a list that contains what we saw,
and the 1-in-20 promise, which is about the plan, would mean nothing.}

\question{Q6.}
Write instructions that another pair could follow to decide about a claim using data.
For a different claim, which of your steps would they have to work out anew?
\answerbox[3.6cm]{(1) State the claim (the coin is fair).
(2) Choose what to compute from the data (the number of heads).
(3) Work out how often each value occurs if the claim is true (the binomial table).
(4) Before seeing the data, list the values that lead to rejecting the claim,
so that a true claim is rejected at most a chosen fraction of the time (1 in 20).
(5) Collect the data, compute the number, and reject the claim if it is on the list.
For a new claim, steps 2--4 must be redone: what to compute, how it behaves if the claim is true, and the list.}

\medskip
The dealer flips: \quad \texttt{H H H T H H H H H H} \quad (9 heads, 1 tail).

\question{Q7.}
Follow your instructions. Do you accuse?
\answerbox[0.9cm]{Yes: 9 heads is on the list $\{0, 1, 9, 10\}$.}

\question{Q8.}
The dealer protests: ``A fair coin can give nine heads!''
Decide which results would be \emph{at least as surprising} as nine heads if the dealer is honest,
and compute how often an honest coin gives one of them.
\answerbox[1.6cm]{Counting both directions (as far from 5 as 9 heads is): 0, 1, 9, or 10 heads,
22 of 1024, about 0.021.
Pairs who count only 9 or 10 heads get 11 of 1024, about 0.011; that matches a suspicion of a coin loaded toward heads.
Either is defensible if chosen before seeing the flips.}

\question{Q9.}
Across many repetitions, what fraction does your number from Q8 describe?
What must be true for that interpretation to hold?
Can it tell us how often dealers who produce this result are honest? Explain.
\answerbox[2.4cm]{The fraction of many 10-flip runs with an honest coin that are at least as surprising as ours.
It needs independent flips with probability 1/2, and ``surprising'' defined without peeking at the data.
It cannot say how often such dealers are honest:
that depends on how many dealers cheat, and by how much, which the calculation never uses.}

\question{Q10.}
Suppose the dealer had flipped 8 heads instead. Following your plan, do you accuse?
Would not accusing prove that the dealer is honest?
\answerbox[1.4cm]{No accusation: 8 is not on the list. That does not prove the coin is fair:
an unfair coin can easily give 8 heads; a coin with heads probability 0.7 is caught only about 15\% of the time.}

\section*{Instructor notes}

The student pages deliberately use no hypothesis-testing vocabulary.
In the debrief, name what the students built:

\smallskip
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.5\textwidth}p{0.4\textwidth}}
  \toprule
  In the worksheet & Standard name \\
  \midrule
  the dealer's claim, ``the coin is fair'' & null hypothesis $H_0$ \\
  ``the coin is unfair'' & alternative $H_1$ (two-sided or one-sided, Q8) \\
  the number of heads (Q2, Q6 step 2) & test statistic \\
  how often each value occurs if the claim is true (Q6 step 3) & null distribution \\
  wrongly accusing an honest dealer (Q3) & type I error, false positive \\
  the limit of 1 time in 20 (Q4) & significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ \\
  the list of results that lead to an accusation (Q4) & rejection region \\
  how often an honest coin is at least as surprising (Q8) & p-value \\
  the instructions (Q6) & the algorithm of a null hypothesis test \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Timing.} About 30 minutes: Q1--Q5 12, Q6 8, Q7--Q10 10.
    Protect the time for Q6, and have two pairs read their instructions aloud.
  \item \textbf{Q1.} Pairs who list only many heads are picturing a coin loaded toward heads;
    ask what a coin loaded toward tails would do. Do not settle it for them: Q8 returns to it.
  \item \textbf{Q3 is a rescue prompt.} Let pairs propose a plan in Q2 before calibrating it.
    The 1-in-20 limit in Q4 is supplied, not derived; say so.
  \item \textbf{Q4--Q5.} The 1-in-20 is a property of the plan over many repeated experiments with a fair coin,
    never the probability that a particular accusation is wrong.
    Plans that accuse on a fixed list of head counts cannot hit 5\% exactly; this one runs at 2.1\%.
  \item \textbf{Q8.} Compare the pairs' choices of ``at least as surprising'':
    both directions gives 22/1024, heads only gives 11/1024.
    This is where two-sided and one-sided tests come from, and why the direction must be chosen before the flips.
  \item \textbf{Q9 and Q10} target the commonest misreadings of a p-value:
    the probability that $H_0$ is true, and ``not rejected'' read as ``proven true''.
    A third worth raising aloud: with a 5\% plan, the fraction of accusations that are wrong is \emph{not} 5\%;
    it depends on how many dealers cheat.
  \item \textbf{Power, at the board after Q10.} Ask students to predict first:
    if the coin really lands heads with probability 0.7, how often does the plan accuse?
    Almost always, about half the time, or rarely?
    Rarely: $P(X = 9) \approx 0.121$, $P(X = 10) \approx 0.028$, $P(X \le 1) \approx 0.0001$, about 0.15 in total.
    That is the \emph{power} of the test against $p = 0.7$; more flips would raise it,
    which leads to sample-size planning on Day 3.
  \item \textbf{Mouse, in the debrief.} A mouse turns left on 9 of 10 T-maze trials:
    can the same table test ``no side preference''?
    Only if the trials act like independent flips with the same probability;
    mice learn, alternate, and perseverate, so step 3 of the instructions needs a different model.
    The same caution applies to cells counted from one tube or one animal.
  \item \textbf{Afternoon.} Students turn their Q6 instructions into Python.
\end{itemize}
